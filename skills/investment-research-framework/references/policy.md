# Policy Layer: 货币 / 财政 / 监管 / 地缘 / 选举 / 国际宏观

## What this layer watches

The policy layer asks: what non-company, non-market-structure force is repricing or about to
reprice equities, sectors, or specific securities? It covers six dimensions, each of which
has its own pipeline from announcement to market impact.

The key discipline in this layer is distinguishing stage of the pipeline:

```
announced intention → proposed rule / legislation → finalized rule / enacted law
→ implementation → first revenue/cost impact → analyst estimate revision → price
```

Markets often react at the "announced intention" stage with maximum uncertainty, then partially
reverse as the actual implemented rule turns out narrower than feared (or broader than hoped).
Understanding where a policy event sits in this pipeline prevents over-reacting to early-stage
announcements and under-reacting to implementation deadlines that are already priced.

---

### 货币政策 (Monetary Policy)

Monetary policy is the most direct driver of equity discount rates and therefore the most
watched of the six dimensions. The primary transmission is through the risk-free rate: as
rates rise, the present value of future earnings falls, compressing multiples — especially for
long-duration growth equities. As rates fall, the reverse occurs.

What to track: the Federal Reserve's target rate path (current vs. futures-implied), the dot
plot for forward rate expectations, QT (quantitative tightening) pace and any discussion of
tapering QT, real rates (nominal minus inflation expectations from TIPS), and the shape of the
yield curve (2Y/10Y spread as a recession signal). For non-US exposure, track ECB, BOJ, PBOC,
and Bank of England policy as relevant to sectors with geographic revenue exposure.

The subtlest monetary signal is often in the language, not the rate decision itself. Fed Chair
press conference tone, minutes, and regional Fed speeches can shift expectations well ahead of
an actual meeting. Watch for changes in the balance of risk language ("inflation is our primary
concern" vs. "we see risks on both sides").

### 财政政策 (Fiscal Policy)

Fiscal policy creates direct winners and losers at the sector and sub-industry level. Budget
acts, supplemental appropriations, tax code changes, and industrial policy programs all reprice
revenue and cost for specific companies.

What to track: the budget and appropriations calendar (continuing resolutions, omnibus spending
bills, defense authorization), any active tax legislation (corporate tax rate changes, R&D
expensing rules, carried interest, capital gains treatment), industrial policy programs (IRA
tax credits for clean energy, CHIPS Act semiconductor subsidies, infrastructure spending
tranches), and the debt ceiling cycle (which affects Treasury issuance pace and therefore
liquidity in the system).

The key question is always: which sectors gain a new revenue source, cost reduction, or demand
stimulus? And which sectors face a new cost or lose a regulatory subsidy?

---

### 监管政策 (Regulatory Policy)

Regulatory actions change the competitive structure and profit pools of industries without
requiring legislation. Antitrust reviews, merger approvals or blocks, export controls, and
industry-specific rules (banking capital requirements, drug pricing negotiations, energy
emissions rules) all operate through agency rulemaking, which has its own multi-year pipeline.

What to track: FTC and DOJ antitrust activity (pending mergers in tech, healthcare,
financials), Commerce Department export control lists (critical to semiconductors and defense
electronics), FDA drug approval calendar (critical to biotech/pharma thesis hands), banking
regulators (Basel III endgame capital rules, CFPB actions), EPA climate rules affecting
utilities and energy.

Regulatory risk is asymmetric: a rule that is proposed but later softened can recover quickly,
but a final rule that restructures a profit pool (e.g., Medicare drug price negotiation) takes
years to fully price in. Note the current rule stage and the next administrative deadline.

### 地缘政治 (Geopolitics)

Geopolitical events affect equity markets primarily through supply chain disruption, commodity
price shocks, sanctions-driven revenue loss, and risk-premium repricing on affected sectors.

What to track: active conflict zones and their proximity to critical supply chains or shipping
lanes (Strait of Hormuz for oil, Red Sea for container shipping, Taiwan Strait for
semiconductors); active or threatened sanctions regimes and the sectors they expose (Russian
energy, Chinese technology, Iranian oil); tariff and trade war escalations (U.S.-China,
U.S.-EU, re-shoring mandates); and critical mineral supply concentrations (cobalt, lithium,
rare earths) that create single-country dependency risk.

The transmission path for geopolitics is usually: supply chain disruption → commodity price
spike or shortage → input cost increase for downstream manufacturers → margin compression →
earnings estimate cuts → multiple compression. The speed of this transmission varies widely.

---

### 选举政治 (Elections)

Elections create policy optionality: they change which policy paths become likely vs. unlikely
over the subsequent 2–4 year legislative horizon. Markets price this optionality — sometimes
correctly, sometimes with substantial over-reaction in either direction.

What to track: polling averages and betting markets for major elections (U.S. presidential,
congressional, key gubernatorial), declared policy platforms with specificity on tax,
regulation, trade, and spending, historical sector sensitivity to each party's policy
priorities (defense under hawkish administrations, clean energy under climate-focused ones,
financials under deregulatory ones), and the legislative math (trifecta vs. divided
government changes what is actually passable).

Be careful about over-extrapolating from electoral uncertainty: markets often move more on
election night than the actual eventual policy impact warrants, creating mean-reversion
opportunities in the affected sectors.

---

### 国际宏观 (International Macro)

U.S. equities do not exist in isolation. Macro conditions in major economies transmit through
trade, currency, and capital flows.

What to track: China growth and stimulus cycle (affects commodities, industrials, luxury),
European growth and ECB policy path (affects multinationals with EU revenue, financials), BOJ
policy and yen carry trade dynamics (periodic shock to risk assets when yen strengthens sharply
and carry unwinds), emerging market currency stress (Brazil, Turkey, Mexico — affects specific
commodities and multinationals), and global trade volumes (Baltic Dry Index, container rates
as leading indicators of industrial and consumer goods demand).

Currency is the first-order transmission: a stronger dollar compresses reported earnings for
U.S. multinationals and creates capital outflow pressure on EM equities. A weaker dollar does
the reverse. Watch the DXY trend and the specific currency pairs relevant to user-tracked
sectors.

## Cross-layer signals

Policy events rarely stay contained to their own layer. A Fed rate decision is a monetary
event that immediately flows into regime (discount rate changes) and into specific sector
leadership (rate-sensitive financials and real estate reprice first). An export control
announcement is a regulatory event that flows into supply chain stress in the sector layer
and into geopolitical risk in the policy layer simultaneously.

When a policy event crosses layers, the compound effect is what matters: a geopolitical shock
that coincides with a Fed tightening cycle and an election with uncertain outcome creates a
multi-layer uncertainty that commands a larger risk premium than any single layer alone.

## When the picture materially shifts

A policy event is material when it moves from "rumor / market speculation" to "official
announcement" or "proposed rule" stage. It becomes more material when implementation
approaches — because that is when companies must update guidance and analysts must revise
estimates. Track the pipeline stage for every active policy event relevant to user-tracked
themes and sectors.

The user's `personal/themes.md` and `personal/watchlist.md` are the filter: a regulatory
action in health care is material if the user tracks biotech; an export control on
semiconductors is material if the user tracks AI infrastructure.

## Reversal cues

- A proposed rule is finalized but turns out narrower than feared: sector that sold off on
  the proposal recovers.
- An election outcome produces a split government that blocks the most market-moving
  legislation: policy uncertainty premium deflates.
- A central bank pivots language more dovishly or hawkishly than expected: the rate-sensitive
  sectors that priced in the opposite direction reverse sharply.
- A geopolitical escalation resolves (ceasefire, negotiated agreement): commodity risk premium
  deflates; affected supply chains normalize.

## What this layer does NOT carry

This layer explains policy mechanisms and their transmission paths. It does not produce
sector heat rankings (that belongs in sector-and-chains) or regime state labels (that belongs
in regime). It does not predict election outcomes or regulatory decisions — it maps the
scenarios and their market consequences.
